\begin{abstract}
Zero Days to Expiration Options (0DTE) have increased in popularity since CBOE released daily SPY expirations in 2022. However, they are
difficult to hedge, and the sources of hedging error are not well understood. This paper takes the Heston model as a starting point
and uses an industry-standard Greek-by-Greek decomposition to measure how much each Greek moves the profit and loss of an
at-the-money delta hedge rebalanced at five-minute intervals, with the variance state read from Cboe's VIX1D index, which is
horizon-matched to a 0DTE contract. Shares are reported two ways: as a signed decomposition of the variance of the realized hedged
P\&L, which is the primary measure, and as gross absolute attribution shares computed from model Greeks, which measure the size of
each term's contribution rather than a split of the realized error. On the variance measure Gamma carries $64.6\%$ and Vega
$31.5\%$; the two together account for almost all of the variance of the hedged P\&L, leaving $5.9\%$ unexplained, and every other
term sits within sampling error of zero. The two largest terms correlate at $\rho = +0.27$ across days, with a $95\%$ interval that
includes zero, so there is no evidence that they cancel within a day. Gamma dominates, absorbing both diffusion convexity and any jump
risk the model cannot identify; a bipower-variation test flags $12$ of the $57$ days as containing jumps, and Gamma's share is
unchanged on the jump-free subsample. On the days when VIX1D travels furthest intraday, Vega's gross share rises from $19.7\%$ to
$42.8\%$. The hedged P\&L drifts negative but not significantly so, at $1.9$ Newey--West standard errors of the mean; the drift
that does appear term by term is a property of the variance proxy rather than a risk premium, since the model's variance exceeds
realized variance on $51$ of $57$ days while VIX1D itself rises mechanically through the session on $55$ of $57$.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
This paper investigates the Greeks as sources of discrete hedging error for at-the-money 0DTE options under Heston.
It takes the standard compensated-Greek decomposition of a delta-hedged option's profit and loss and applies it to
at-the-money 0DTE calls, hedged at five-minute frequency on real SPY data with the variance state taken from the VIX1D index. The
contribution is a term-by-term measurement of how much each Greek moves the hedged profit and loss, and how that ranking shifts
with the volatility regime. Two measures are reported and they answer different questions: a signed decomposition of the variance
of the realized hedged P\&L, which is the primary measure, and a gross absolute attribution share, which records how much a term
moves without regard to whether that movement survives into the realized error. The distinction is developed in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Gamma dominates in every regime; what the volatility regime changes is Vega, which becomes a material
source of variance in the hedged P\&L on the days VIX1D travels furthest, and which also acquires a drift that Section~\ref{sec:results}
attributes to the proxy rather than to the market. The endogeneity of that sort is discussed alongside the result.

\subsection{Black--Scholes Model and Greeks}
The Black--Scholes model, introduced by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes (1973)~\cite{blackscholes1973} and Robert Merton (1973)~\cite{merton1973},
provides a framework for pricing European options in a closed form that Heston expands on. The issue with Black--Scholes
is that it assumes constant volatility, which is not the case in real markets, especially for 0DTE options. The Heston
model, explained below, allows for stochastic volatility. Black--Scholes also introduced the concept of Greeks, which are
sensitivities of the option price to various parameters. They are listed below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Delta ($\Delta$): Sensitivity of the option price to changes in the underlying asset price.
    \item Gamma ($\Gamma$): Sensitivity of Delta to changes in the underlying asset price.
    \item Vega ($\nu$): Sensitivity of the option price to changes in volatility.
    \item Theta ($\Theta$): Sensitivity of the option price to the passage of time.
    \item  Charm ($\frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial t}$): Sensitivity of Delta to the passage of time.
    \item Vanna ($\frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma}$): Sensitivity of Delta to changes in volatility.
    \item Volga, also called Vomma ($\frac{\partial \nu}{\partial \sigma}$): Sensitivity of Vega to changes in volatility.
    \item Veta ($\frac{\partial \nu}{\partial t}$): Sensitivity of Vega to the passage of time.
    \item Time convexity ($\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial t^2}$): Second-order sensitivity of the option price to the passage of time.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Related Literature}


The decomposition in this paper relies heavily on various prior works. The Heston model \cite{heston1993}
is used as the underlying model for the option pricing and hedging found later in the paper. Along with that, the Euler--Maruyama
scheme \cite{Maruyama1955}, in the standard form given by Kloeden and Platen~\cite{kloedenplaten1992}, is used to simulate the
stochastic differential equations used in the implementation of the paper. The Greek decomposition follows Bergomi~\cite{bergomi2016}. Four of the seven terms used here are his, the Gamma, Volga, Vanna, and Vega terms, as is the compensator method that subtracts from each term the drift it earns under the model, so that what is left is the break-even quantity. The three remaining terms, Charm, Veta, and time convexity, are of order $\Delta t$ and are the 0DTE-specific addition: they are negligible at the maturities Bergomi treats, but the expansion here is carried to second order across a single session, where they do not vanish.
Specifically, his ``break-even'' logic is used to attribute the hedging error to each Greek.
Bergomi's logic is the implied vs. realized volatility, where dominating volatility leads to price dominating the true price, and the Gamma variance drives the hedging error.
This implied-versus-realized logic was formalized by El~Karoui, Jeanblanc-Picqu\'e, and Shreve (1998)~\cite{elkaroui1998}, who show that the P\&L of a delta hedge run at the wrong volatility is the dollar-Gamma-weighted spread between the hedging and realized variances.
Bakshi and Kapadia (2003)~\cite{bakshi2003} take that spread to the data, running delta-hedged S\&P 500 option positions over horizons of days to months and finding their gains systematically negative, which they read as evidence of a negative market volatility risk premium. The single-session analogue is measured in Section~\ref{sec:results} and does not reproduce it: the mean daily hedged P\&L is negative but statistically insignificant, and the drifts that do appear term by term are traced there to the variance proxy rather than to a premium.

The result that discrete-hedging error scales as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$, where $N$ is the number of rebalances, originates with
Boyle and Emanuel (1980)~\cite{boyleemanuel1980}, who derive the variance of the hedging error of a discretely adjusted option
position. Derman and Kamal \cite{kamal1999} give the practitioner treatment of that result and the closest antecedent to this paper: a guide to
hedging errors when one cannot hedge continuously, written for a trading desk rather than for a journal. Bertsimas--Kogan--Lo
\cite{bertsimas2000} approach the same problem independently and from the continuous-time side,
finding that discrete tracking error grows with the granularity constant, $g$, which is built from accumulated dollar gamma. The formal contribution of Bertsimas et al. is
the expression $\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}$; they also show that the kink in the call payoff does not break this convergence rate, but rather inflates $g$.
Dim, Eraker, and Vilkov (2023) \cite{dimeraker2023}
study how 0DTE order imbalances and the resulting dealer gamma exposure propagate into market volatility. That channel is microstructural; the attribution
here is at the contract level. No prior paper gives a term-by-term hedging-error attribution for 0DTE options under Heston.


\subsection{Choices and Rationale}
The Heston model was chosen for its stochastic volatility and easily implementable characteristic function. Also, the Heston parameters
are round, arbitrarily chosen values rather than calibrated estimates, except for $\eta$ (see the Methodology section for further information). Their specific combination violates the Feller condition,
so the variance process can reach zero; the simulation therefore uses a full-truncation Euler--Maruyama scheme~\cite{lord2010}, which floors the variance at
zero at each step.

\subsection{Heston Model Derivation}
The derivation here follows both Gatheral (2006)~\cite{gatheral2006} and Heston (1993)~\cite{heston1993}. The derivation starts with the Heston model's stochastic differential equations
for the underlying asset price $S$ and its variance $v$:
\[
dS = \mu S\,dt + \sqrt{v}\,S\,dX,
\qquad
dv = \kappa(\eta - v)\,dt + \epsilon \sqrt{v}\,dW,
\qquad
dX\,dW = \rho\,dt,
\]
There are two sources of randomness here, the price shock $dX$ and the variance shock $dW$, so the hedge takes two instruments: the stock, and a second option $V_1$ with its own exposure to the variance.
Take the portfolio, $\Pi$, with the option $V$ by shorting $\Delta S$, which is the
dollar value of the stock shorted. Short $\Delta_1$ shares of $V_1$ as well:
\[
\Pi = V - \Delta S - \Delta_1 V_1 .
\]
Applying It\^o's lemma to $V$ gives its change over an instant:
\[
dV = \left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
+ \frac{1}{2} v S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
+ \rho \epsilon v S \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial v}
+ \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 v \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v^2} \right) dt
+ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS
+ \frac{\partial V}{\partial v}\,dv,
\]
Apply the same expansion to $V_1$, and substitute both into $d\Pi$ to obtain the following equation:
\begin{align*}
d\Pi &= \left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
+ \frac{1}{2} v S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
+ \rho \epsilon v S \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial v}
+ \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 v \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v^2} \right) dt \\
&\quad {} - \Delta_1 \left( \frac{\partial V_1}{\partial t}
+ \frac{1}{2} v S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V_1}{\partial S^2}
+ \rho \epsilon v S \frac{\partial^2 V_1}{\partial S\,\partial v}
+ \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 v \frac{\partial^2 V_1}{\partial v^2} \right) dt \\
&\quad {} + \left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta_1 \frac{\partial V_1}{\partial S} - \Delta \right) dS
+ \left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial v} - \Delta_1 \frac{\partial V_1}{\partial v} \right) dv.
\end{align*}
The last line shows how to remove the risk: set $\Delta_1$ to cancel the $dv$ exposure, and $\Delta$ to cancel the $dS$ exposure,
\[
\Delta_1 = \frac{\partial V/\partial v}{\partial V_1/\partial v},
\qquad
\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta_1 \frac{\partial V_1}{\partial S},
\]
leaving a riskless portfolio. No-arbitrage then forces $\Pi$ to earn the risk-free rate, $d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt$. Because the same argument holds for any option, the terms involving $V$ and the terms involving $V_1$ must each equal a common function of $(S, v, t)$; following Heston, that function is written as $-\nu_{\mathrm{Q}}$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\partial V/\partial v} \biggl(
&\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
+ \frac{1}{2} v S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
+ \rho \epsilon v S \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial v} \\
&{} + \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 v \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v^2}
+ rS \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
- rV \biggr) = -\nu_{\mathrm{Q}},
\end{aligned}
\]
where
\[
\nu_{\mathrm{Q}} \equiv \kappa(\eta - v) - \lambda v
\]
is the risk-neutral drift of the variance, with $\lambda$ the market price of volatility risk. Rearranging gives the Heston pricing PDE:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
+ \frac{1}{2} v S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
+ \rho \epsilon v S \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial v}
+ \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 v \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v^2} \\
&\quad {} + rS \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
+ \big[\kappa(\eta - v) - \lambda v\big] \frac{\partial V}{\partial v}
- rV = 0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\section{The Error Model}
\subsection{Derivation}

The Error model is derived from the Heston model; the two coincide in the continuous limit and diverge as the hedge becomes discrete.
The Heston model assumes continuous hedging; therefore, $\Delta t \to 0$. However, in practice, hedging is done at discrete intervals,
 which introduces hedging error. To decompose said error, the following model is proposed based on Bergomi's compensated-Greek decomposition, adding the discrete-time terms (charm, veta, and time convexity) that
 arise from expanding the profit and loss to second order in $\Delta t$~\cite{bergomi2016}:
\[
\Delta\Pi = \Delta V - \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,\Delta S
- r\,\Big( V - \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,S \Big)\,\Delta t .
\]
\begin{align*}
\Delta\Pi &= \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,\Delta t
+ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,\Delta S
+ \frac{\partial V}{\partial v}\,\Delta v \\
&\quad {} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\,(\Delta S)^2
+ \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v^2}\,(\Delta v)^2
+ \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial v}\,\Delta S\,\Delta v \\
&\quad {} + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial t}\,\Delta S\,\Delta t
+ \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v\,\partial t}\,\Delta v\,\Delta t
+ \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial t^2}\,(\Delta t)^2 \\
&\quad {} - \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,\Delta S
- r\,\Big( V - \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,S \Big)\,\Delta t .
\end{align*}
\[
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = rV - rS\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
- \frac{1}{2} v S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
- \rho \epsilon v S\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial v}
- \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 v\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v^2}
- \nu_{\mathrm{Q}}\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial v} ,
\]
\begin{align*}
\Delta\Pi &= \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\big[ (\Delta S)^2 - v S^2\,\Delta t \big]
+ \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v^2}\big[ (\Delta v)^2 - \epsilon^2 v\,\Delta t \big] \\
&\quad {} + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial v}\big[ \Delta S\,\Delta v - \rho \epsilon v S\,\Delta t \big]
+ \frac{\partial V}{\partial v}\big[ \Delta v - \nu_{\mathrm{Q}}\,\Delta t \big] \\
&\quad {} + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S\,\partial t}\,\Delta S\,\Delta t
+ \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v\,\partial t}\,\Delta v\,\Delta t
+ \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial t^2}\,(\Delta t)^2 .
\end{align*}
Not every term above is discretization error. Summed over a day, the Gamma, Vanna, and Volga
compensators vanish as the hedging interval shrinks, since realized quadratic variation converges to the model's $\int v S^2\,dt$ and
likewise for the cross and variance brackets, while the charm, veta, and time-convexity terms are of order $\Delta t$ or smaller.
Those six terms measure finite-mesh error: the price of rebalancing every five minutes instead of continuously. The Vega term
behaves otherwise. Its sum converges to $\int \frac{\partial V}{\partial v}\,\epsilon\sqrt{v}\,dW$, a random quantity that
survives any rebalancing frequency. That is the variance risk a delta-only hedge leaves open, since the hedge holds no second
option. The Heston derivation above removed this risk with the hedging option $V_1$; the experiment below deliberately holds only
the stock, as most 0DTE hedgers do, so the Vega term tracks unhedged risk rather than finite-mesh error.

\subsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
The Heston parameters were not calibrated to data: apart from $\eta$, they are round, arbitrarily chosen values.
$\eta$ was set from the VIX1D-implied variance in the sample. Given that information, the results in the
next section are not to be taken as absolute, but rather as a proof of concept. The parameters used are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\kappa = 3.0$ (rate of mean reversion)
    \item $\eta = 0.0160$ (long-run variance; the sample average of $(\mathrm{VIX1D}/100)^2$ across the 57 days)
    \item $\epsilon = 1.0$ (volatility of volatility)
    \item $\rho = -0.7$ (correlation between asset and variance)
    \item $\lambda = 0$ (market price of volatility risk)
    \item $r = 0.045$ (risk-free rate, used in the financing term of the hedged P\&L)
\end{itemize}

Two of these are uncalibrated free choices worth probing, $\kappa$ and $\epsilon$, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports a sweep over both. The mean-reversion rate $\kappa$ is expected to be
close to inert on this horizon: it enters only through $\nu_{\mathrm{Q}}\,\Delta t = \kappa(\eta - v)\,\Delta t$, which for
$\kappa = 3$ and a five-minute step is of order $10^{-6}$, against a typical per-bar $\Delta v$ two orders
of magnitude larger. The vol-of-vol $\epsilon$ has no such protection, since it scales the Volga
compensator as $\epsilon^2 v\,\Delta t$ and the Vanna compensator as $\rho\epsilon v S\,\Delta t$. The baseline is set at
$\epsilon = 1.0$ rather than at the lower $\epsilon = 0.5$ deliberately. Calibrated short-horizon index vol-of-vol is
commonly well above $0.5$, and the sweep in Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that the low value is the one most
flattering to the headline Gamma share, so reporting it as the baseline would overstate the result. The sample itself
supports the choice: regressing the VIX1D variance increments in the form $\Delta v/(\sqrt{v}\sqrt{\Delta t})$ gives a
realized vol-of-vol of $\hat{\epsilon} = 0.90$, close to the chosen baseline and nowhere near $0.5$. The same exercise
gives a realized price--variance correlation of $\hat{\rho} = -0.73$ against the assumed $-0.7$, so of the two
uncalibrated diffusion parameters $\rho$ turns out to have been guessed well and $\epsilon$ badly.

Five-minute SPY data was used as a proxy for the underlying asset; the pipeline rescales each day to open at 1, so the results are invariant to the units of the price series. The sample covers 57 trading days, from May 11th, 2026 through July 31st, 2026.
That window holds 60 weekdays, of which Memorial Day, Juneteenth, and the observed Independence Day holiday are closed, which is where the count of 57 comes from.
The window is set by data availability rather than by choice: the intraday feed serves five-minute index data only for the trailing sixty trading days, so a longer or earlier sample is not obtainable from this source.
The option prices were computed using the Heston model, and the hedging error was measured by rebalancing the delta hedge at each five-minute bar.
The contributions of each Greek to the overall hedged profit and loss were then analyzed and their results are found
in the following section. Better data would use actual option pricing instead of proxies, but option data is expensive and currently a limitation
for this paper.

In terms of the derivation itself, the specific terms above were chosen to allow
for all the terms to be expressed as Greeks, and have a discrete and nondiscrete component. The discrete component is the difference between the actual change
in the underlying asset and the expected change, while the nondiscrete component is the expected change itself. The discrete component, following Bergomi's 2016 book,
is what contributes to the
hedging error, while the nondiscrete component is what is expected to be hedged away. If the limit is taken, the model collapses back to the Heston model for every term except Vega:
the other compensators go to zero with the hedging interval, while the Vega term converges to the unhedged variance risk described above.

In the implementation, the trapezoid rule was used to compute the integrals in the Heston pricer, and the Greeks were computed using finite differences.
The code is available in the accompanying Python file on GitHub. The $\omega$ grid begins at $10^{-8}$ to avoid the $1/(i\omega)$ singularity at zero, and the upper limit of
the integral is set to 2500 to ensure convergence for 0DTE options. The number of points in the grid is set to 2048, which is enough for the reported prices to
stabilize. If using a stronger computer, the number of points can be increased to improve accuracy, but at the cost of longer computation time. With testing, 
it has been found that if $\lambda$ is set to 0, then the results are not sensitive to the number of points in the grid, as the integrals converge quickly. 
However, if $\lambda$ is nonzero, then the results can be sensitive to the number of points in the grid, as the integrals converge more slowly. Also, setting $\lambda$ to a nonzero value
can introduce numerical instability in the integrals, as the integrands can become highly oscillatory, therefore making the pricer and the vega compensator disagree.

Two distinct experiments appear below, and the distinction governs how each residual should be read. The empirical study of
Section~\ref{sec:results} is not simulated at all: $\Delta S$ is the realized five-minute change in the observed underlying and
$\Delta v$ is the realized change in the squared VIX1D. The validation of Section~\ref{sec:validation} is simulated, using the
Euler--Maruyama scheme to generate paths from the Heston dynamics.

On the simulated paths the price is carried in levels rather than log prices. An Euler step
$\Delta S = rS\,\Delta t + \sqrt{v}\,S\,\Delta X$ has conditional variance $vS^2\,\Delta t$, which is exactly the quantity the
Gamma compensator $(\Delta S)^2 - vS^2\,\Delta t$ subtracts. Because the paths are advanced in several sub-steps per hedge bar,
simulating in levels keeps that compensator mean-zero up to an $O(\Delta t^2)$ correction, of the same order as the Taylor
truncation itself, so any residual left in the \emph{simulated} test reflects second-order error rather than a mismatch
between how the paths were generated and how the error was decomposed. A log-Euler step would reintroduce such a mismatch through
the $-\frac{1}{2}v$ Itô correction, which the level scheme does not carry.

No such guarantee is available on real data, though the gap narrows considerably once the variance proxy is horizon-matched. There
$\Delta S$ is whatever the market did, while $vS^2\,\Delta t$ is built from a VIX1D-derived variance that was never fitted to the
realized path. Summed within each day, the realized quadratic variation $\sum (\Delta S)^2$ averages $4.75 \times 10^{-5}$ across
the sample, against $6.32 \times 10^{-5}$ for the model's $\sum v S^2\,\Delta t$. The model quantity is about a third larger than
the realized one, and it exceeds it on 51 of the 57 days. The Gamma compensator is therefore not mean-zero on the empirical sample
but reliably negative, and because an at-the-money call is long Gamma the decomposition's largest term carries a systematic
negative drift. The residual reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} accordingly mixes second-order Taylor truncation with error in
the variance proxy, and the two residuals in this paper measure different things and should not be compared directly.


The variance path is read off Cboe's VIX1D index, introduced in 2023 and computed from zero- to one-day SPY
options~\cite{cboe2023vix1d}. The choice is deliberate and it matters. The thirty-day VIX is the wrong horizon for this
experiment: $\mathrm{VIX}^2$ is the expected \emph{average} variance over the coming thirty days, whereas the model calls for the
instantaneous spot variance $v_t$, and because variance mean-reverts a thirty-day forward average is pulled toward the long-run
level and barely moves within a session. Used as $v_t$ it leaves the Vega, Vanna, and Volga terms only lightly exercised, and it
overstates the variance an expiring contract actually faces. VIX1D is matched to the maturity the 0DTE option lives on and moves
freely intraday, and the results below are built on it.

Two caveats attach to the substitution, one inherited and one new. The inherited one is that $\mathrm{VIX1D}^2$ remains a
risk-neutral quantity rather than a physical one, and it embeds a variance risk premium that the model, with $\lambda = 0$, does
not capture. The new one is specific to the one-day horizon and is the more serious of the two. VIX1D measures expected variance
over the coming twenty-four hours, so as a session advances its window rolls off the remainder of today and onto the overnight gap
and the following session. For a contract expiring at today's close the remaining variance should decay toward zero over the
session; VIX1D instead climbs, averaging $10.90$ at 09:30 and $13.93$ at 15:55 across the sample, rising on 55 of the 57 days at a
Newey--West $t$ of $+8.5$. The index is horizon-matched in name, but its intraday path carries a deterministic roll-up that the
model reads as genuine variance news.

The size of the mismatch can be stated exactly. A contract expiring at today's close has $390 - 5b$ minutes of life left at the
$b$th five-minute bar, while VIX1D always looks forward a fixed $1440$ minutes, so the fraction of the index's window that bears
on the contract at all falls from $27.1\%$ at the open to $18.8\%$ at 11:30, $10.4\%$ at 13:30, and $0.3\%$ in the final bar. By
the close, essentially all of what VIX1D is measuring is tomorrow. This is why the roll is mechanical rather than incidental, and
it also bounds what any correction can achieve: an adjustment can remove the average climb, but it cannot recover information the
index does not carry. Section~\ref{sec:detrend} removes the climb and shows that doing so eliminates the Vega term's drift and
leaves the attribution intact. The properly matched instrument would be the implied variance of the expiring contract itself,
read off 0DTE option prices at each bar, which requires the option data this paper does not have.

\section{Validation \& Results}
\label{sec:validation}
According to Bertsimas, Kogan, and Lo (2000)~\cite{bertsimas2000}, the granularity constant $g$, which measures how difficult an option is to hedge
in discrete time and sets the size of the tracking error, blows up in the terminal interval. The Taylor expansion also
fails as the option approaches expiry, which is one candidate explanation for the $20.2\%$ gross residual reported in the next subsection.

The decomposition algebra itself was checked away from that corner. A 60-day option was simulated under the Heston engine and
hedged daily, with the position closed 20 days before expiry so that no step straddles the payoff kink and every Greek stays
smooth; there the seven terms reproduced the simulated hedged P\&L to within $1.74\%$, at a correlation of $0.9998$. That is a test of
whether the expansion is correct, and it passes.

The terminal-interval explanation was then tested directly, and on the real data rather than by analogy to the 60-day run. The
same 57 days were hedged the same way, but the position was closed a number of bars before the bell and marked out at the pricer
value instead of being held into expiry; every other element of the experiment is unchanged, so distance from expiry is the only
thing that varies. The gross residual falls monotonically and steeply as the position is closed earlier: $20.2\%$ held to expiry,
$17.7\%$ closed one five-minute bar early, $17.0\%$ two bars early, $16.0\%$ three bars early, $14.0\%$ half an hour early, $10.5\%$
one hour early, and $6.2\%$ two hours early. Closing merely one bar before the bell removes two and a half points of residual,
closing thirty minutes early removes nearly a third of it, and closing two hours early removes better than two thirds. The
unexplained variance share falls along the same ladder, from $5.9\%$ held to expiry to $1.0\%$ two hours early. The residual is therefore concentrated in the final intervals,
exactly as the granularity constant blowing up in the terminal interval would predict. This is a controlled version of the
comparison the 60-day run could only gesture at: the maturity, hedge frequency, and data source are all held fixed, so the fall
can be attributed to distance from expiry alone.

The 60-day simulation of the previous paragraph remains useful as an algebra check but is not the terminal-interval test: it
differs from the 0DTE study in maturity, hedge frequency, and data source at once, and by the argument of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} its residual is not even the same quantity, being second-order truncation on paths where the Gamma
compensator is mean-zero to leading order, whereas the empirical residual also contains variance-proxy error. The direct truncation
test above is the controlled statement; the 60-day run is corroborating context.



\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}
The decomposition was tested on 57 full trading days of five-minute data (78 bars per day, so 77 hedge intervals). Each day is rescaled to open at 1, the variance path is read off the squared VIX1D, and the option is a fresh at-the-money 0DTE call re-formed each morning and delta-hedged at every bar. Summing the seven compensated terms reproduces the realized hedged P\&L to within a $20.2\%$ gross residual, defined as the mean absolute unexplained P\&L across the 57 days divided by the mean absolute daily P\&L. That metric is the harsher of the two reported here; measured against the \emph{variance} of the realized hedged P\&L, which is the decomposition the rest of this section leads with, the unexplained share is $5.9\%$. The mean daily hedged P\&L is $-0.00019$ in the units where the underlying opens at 1, with a standard deviation across days of $0.00087$.

That mean is not statistically distinguishable from zero, and the reason it is not is more informative than the mean itself. Daily P\&L is serially dependent on this sample, with a Ljung--Box $Q(10)$ of $25.9$ ($p = 0.004$), so the independent-sampling standard error understates uncertainty and all inference below uses Newey--West errors at the automatic lag $L = 3$. With a standard deviation of $0.00087$ across $D = 57$ days the Newey--West standard error of the mean is $0.00010$, placing the average daily loss $1.9$ standard errors below zero, which does not clear conventional significance; 40 of the 57 days lose money. The hedge does not lose persistently on this data.

Two large term-level drifts sit underneath that null, and they nearly cancel. The Gamma term averages $-4.58 \times 10^{-4}$ per day at a Newey--West $t$ of $-6.2$, negative on 49 of 57 days, and the Vega term averages $+3.77 \times 10^{-4}$ at $t = +6.5$, positive on 45. Neither is a risk premium. The Gamma drift is the variance input, measured in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}: the $vS^2\,\Delta t$ subtracted inside the Gamma compensator runs about a third larger than the variance the underlying actually realized and exceeds it on 51 of the 57 days, and an at-the-money call is long Gamma, so the position pays that spread on most days in the sample. The Vega drift is the VIX1D roll-up of Section~\ref{sec:methodology}: the proxy climbs through the session on 55 of 57 days, the Vega term is $\partial V/\partial v\,[\Delta v - \nu_{\mathrm{Q}}\Delta t]$, and a variance input that rises by construction hands that term a positive mean by construction. Both drifts are properties of the measurement rather than of the market, they point in opposite directions, and the near-zero net is the coincidence of two proxy errors rather than evidence that a 0DTE delta hedge breaks even. This sample cannot speak to the variance risk premium at all.

Table~\ref{tab:attribution} reports each term two ways, and the two measures answer different questions. The \emph{variance share} is a signed decomposition of the variance of the realized hedged P\&L: term $i$'s share is $\mathrm{cov}(\text{term}_i,\,\Delta\Pi)/\mathrm{var}(\Delta\Pi)$, so the seven terms and the unexplained remainder sum to $100\%$ by construction and a term earns share only insofar as it moves \emph{with} the error actually realized. This is the measure that answers where the hedging error goes, and it is the primary one here. The \emph{gross share} is the model-attribution measure carried by earlier drafts of this work: each term's average absolute daily contribution as a fraction of the sum across all seven, computed from model Greeks. It is weaker than the phrase ``where the error goes'' suggests, because it records how much a term \emph{moves} and not how much of the realized error it accounts for; it is retained because it is the natural measure for the regime comparison, where the question is which Greek is being exercised rather than which is being realized.

The gap between the two measures is where a naive reading goes wrong, and Vanna shows it plainly: it takes $9.2\%$ of the gross movement but a variance share of $-1.5\%$ with an interval spanning zero, so it moves without contributing to the error that is realized. Across the 57 days the Gamma and Vega terms correlate at $\rho = +0.27$, with a $95\%$ interval of $[-0.13,\,+0.58]$, so there is no evidence that the two terms carrying nearly all of the movement cancel within a day. The strongest offsetting pair anywhere in the matrix is Vega against Veta at $\rho = -0.71$, but Veta's daily standard deviation is smaller than Vega's by a factor of sixty-eight, so that cancellation is immaterial in P\&L terms. In the table, Vega, Vanna, Volga, and Veta denote derivatives with respect to the variance $v$, not the volatility $\sigma$ of Section~1; the two are related by chain rule but are not numerically identical. Both overall columns carry a percentile-bootstrap 95\% interval over days ($B = 10{,}000$ resamples of the 57 days, shares recomputed on each). Gamma leads on both measures and in every regime, as one would expect of an option hedged only in the underlying, but it no longer dominates the way the thirty-day proxy made it appear: half the total absolute term movement and two-thirds of the realized P\&L variance sit in the $\frac{1}{2}\,\partial^2 V/\partial S^2$ term. Vega is the second term and it is not a minor one, taking $34.7\%$ of the gross movement and $31.5\%$ of the variance. A horizon-matched variance proxy moves enough within a session that the variance-facing part of the decomposition becomes a first-order object rather than a rounding error, and that is the single largest change from a thirty-day proxy.

What the regime changes is the balance between the two. Vega's gross share rises from $19.7\%$ on calm days to $42.8\%$ on turbulent ones while Gamma's falls from $62.9\%$ to $44.9\%$, and in the turbulent tercile the two are within about two points of each other. The signed reading does not track that sort cleanly. Vega's variance share goes $10.2\%$, $26.7\%$, $24.0\%$ across the terciles and Gamma's goes $77.2\%$, $65.5\%$, $73.9\%$: both move, neither is monotone, and with nineteen days per bucket the middle and turbulent figures are not separable. What does move monotonically is Vega's correlation with the realized P\&L, $+0.27$, $+0.69$, $+0.71$. Two claims carried by earlier drafts of this work do not survive the horizon-matched proxy and should be discarded. Vega's dispersion does not grow faster than Gamma's across the sort: its standard deviation across days grows by a factor of $1.4$ against Gamma's $1.5$. And its mean is no longer indistinguishable from zero, running at Newey--West $t$ of $-0.4$, $+9.0$, and $+9.7$ across the three buckets. That drift is the roll-up of Section~\ref{sec:methodology} rather than a market fact: the sort is on how far VIX1D travelled, VIX1D travels upward on 55 of 57 days, and so the turbulent bucket is disproportionately the bucket in which the proxy climbed most. Turbulence buys Vega a larger share of the movement; the accompanying drift is an artifact of how the variance was measured.

Vanna is the one term whose two measures disagree in sign, at $9.2\%$ gross against $-1.5\%$ of variance with an interval spanning zero: it is exercised without contributing to the realized error. Charm, time convexity, Volga, and Veta stay within a few percent on both measures throughout, and by the bootstrap intervals in Table~\ref{tab:attribution} the Charm and time-convexity shares are too close to rank against each other.

The regime pattern needs one caveat, and it is sharper here than it was under a thirty-day proxy. The Vega term is $\partial V/\partial v\,[\Delta v - \nu_{\mathrm{Q}}\Delta t]$, so it is larger by construction on days when the variance proxy moved; sorting days by exactly that quantity and then observing that Vega's share rises is partly circular. To check that the swing is a feature of turbulent markets rather than of the sort, the same 57 days were re-sorted into terciles by the absolute intraday move in the underlying, $|\Delta S|$, a criterion that does not appear in the Vega term. The swing survives: Gamma's gross share runs $55.5\%$ to $42.3\%$ from the calmest to the most active tercile, and Vega's $30.6\%$ to $43.1\%$. The effect is attenuated relative to the $|\Delta\mathrm{VIX1D}|$ sort but clearly present, so it is not an artifact of the sorting variable. The control is also a better one than it was: the two sorts correlate at $0.55$ by Pearson and only $0.25$ by Spearman on this sample, against $0.80$ under the thirty-day proxy, so the underlying's move and the variance proxy's move are now close to independent orderings of the sample. The variance shares under the $|\Delta S|$ sort are again not monotone, at $71.4\%$, $39.4\%$, $71.4\%$ for Gamma and $13.9\%$, $48.5\%$, $43.8\%$ for Vega, which is a further reason to read the regime result as a statement about which Greek is exercised rather than about which carries the realized error.

Because Heston carries no jump term, any intraday jump enters the decomposition only through $(\Delta S)^2$ and is absorbed into the Gamma term, so the Gamma share reported above is a diffusion-plus-jump quantity. Separating the two inside the term requires a jump model and is beyond this decomposition, but the contamination can be bounded from the same five-minute bars. Following Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2006)~\cite{bnsjump2006}, realized variance $\sum r_i^2$ estimates total quadratic variation while bipower variation $\mu_1^{-2}\sum |r_i||r_{i-1}|$ is robust to finite-activity jumps, so their difference isolates the jump contribution; the ratio statistic is standardized by tri-power quarticity in the form given by Huang and Tauchen (2005)~\cite{huangtauchen2005}. On the 77 five-minute returns of each session the test flags 12 of the 57 days as containing jumps at the $5\%$ level and 3 at the $1\%$ level, and the average relative jump contribution $\max(RV - BV,\,0)/RV$ is $7.1\%$.

Re-running the attribution on the 45 jump-free days alone leaves the result intact. Gamma's gross share is $50.6\%$ against $50.0\%$ on the full sample, Vega's $34.4\%$ against $34.7\%$, and the gross residual $19.9\%$ against $20.2\%$; on the variance measure Gamma takes $59.3\%$ against $64.6\%$ and Vega $33.3\%$ against $31.5\%$, with the unexplained share rising to $9.4\%$. Gamma's dominance is therefore not produced by the days on which jumps are detectable, and the headline survives on a clean subsample. Two limits on that conclusion should be stated. The subsample has 45 days rather than 57, so the intervals widen. More importantly the test discriminates weakly on this sample: the 12 flagged days on their own give a Gamma gross share of $47.8\%$, close to the jump-free figure, so the check bounds the contamination rather than demonstrating that jumps are irrelevant. What it rules out is the reading in which the Gamma share is an artifact of a handful of jump days.

The two remaining robustness questions are the mean-reversion rate $\kappa$ and the vol-of-vol $\epsilon$, both chosen rather than calibrated. Each was swept over three values with all other parameters and the data held fixed; Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} reports both. The two behave very differently. Varying $\kappa$ across $\{1, 3, 5\}$ moves no share by as much as a third of a percentage point: the overall Gamma gross share runs $49.9\%$, $50.0\%$, $50.2\%$ and the variance share is $64.6\%$ in all three cases. This confirms numerically what the magnitude argument of Section~\ref{sec:methodology} anticipated, that on a five-minute 0DTE horizon $\kappa$ enters only through the negligible $\nu_{\mathrm{Q}}\,\Delta t$ term and cannot affect the attribution. Varying $\epsilon$ across $\{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ is a different story. Gamma remains the largest term throughout, but its gross share falls from $52.1\%$ at $\epsilon = 0.5$ to $41.5\%$ at $\epsilon = 2.0$ and its variance share from $69.1\%$ to $55.3\%$. The gross share it sheds goes to Vanna and Volga, the cross- and second-variance terms that scale in $\epsilon$. Vega's variance share is the one quantity in the table that $\epsilon$ leaves alone, sitting at $31.4\%$, $31.5\%$, and $30.8\%$ across the sweep even as its gross share drifts down from $36.1\%$ to $29.1\%$; the part of the realized error that Vega explains is a property of the data rather than of the vol-of-vol. The regime pattern of Table~\ref{tab:attribution} holds in every row, with Gamma falling and Vega rising from the calm to the turbulent tercile. The gross residual falls slightly as $\epsilon$ grows, from $21.6\%$ to $18.5\%$. The reading of the headline is that Gamma carries between two-fifths and one-half of the term movement and between five-ninths and seven-tenths of the realized variance, with the precise figure set by $\epsilon$ and not by $\kappa$, and that Vega is a stable second on the measure that matters.

\subsection{Removing the Variance Proxy's Intraday Roll}
\label{sec:detrend}
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} argued that VIX1D climbs through the session for a mechanical reason and that the climb is what
gives the Vega term its drift. That argument can be tested rather than asserted, by removing the climb and rerunning everything.

The adjustment is the simplest one that does the job. Averaging across the 57 days gives a mean intraday path $m(b)$ at each
five-minute bar $b$, rising from $10.90$ index points at the open to $13.93$ at the close. Each day's level is then reduced by the
amount the average day had gained by that bar,
\[
\widetilde{L}(d,b) = L(d,b) - \big[\,m(b) - m(0)\,\big],
\]
so the subtraction is zero at the open and $3.03$ points by the close, and $\widetilde{L}^2/100^2$ replaces $v$ everywhere. Only
the variance input changes; prices, realized P\&L, and the pricing machinery are untouched. The adjustment removes only what every
day shares, so the cross-day dispersion of the net move is unaffected, its standard deviation staying at $2.59$ points, while the
mean net move falls from $+3.03$ points to zero and the count of rising days falls from 55 of 57 to 24. A day that gained the
typical three points now shows no net move, which is the correct reading, while the most turbulent day in the sample,
2026-06-05, still gains $11.35$ points of the $14.38$ it appeared to gain. A multiplicative variant,
$\tilde{v}(d,b) = v(d,b)\,[m(0)/m(b)]^2$, is reported alongside to show the conclusions do not turn on the functional form.

Table~\ref{tab:detrend} settles the diagnosis. The Vega term's drift, which runs at a Newey--West $t$ of $+6.48$ on the raw proxy
and at $+9.0$ and $+9.7$ in the two most active terciles, falls to $+0.56$ overall and to nothing in every tercile once the roll
is removed. The roll was not one contributor to that drift, it was the whole of it. The Gamma term's drift weakens in the same
motion, from $-6.21$ to $-2.15$, and the model's quadratic variation stops overshooting the realized quantity by a third, falling
to within seven percent of it and exceeding it on 40 days rather than 51. Both of the large term-level drifts reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results} are therefore properties of the proxy, and the second of them is largely the same defect as the first.

What does not change is the attribution. Gamma's gross share moves from $50.0\%$ to $46.7\%$ and its variance share from $64.6\%$
to $63.2\%$; Vega's gross share is unmoved at $34.8\%$ against $34.7\%$. The regime pattern survives, with Vega's gross share in
the turbulent tercile at $46.7\%$ against $42.8\%$ on the raw proxy. The decomposition also reconstructs the realized P\&L better
once the artifact is gone, the unexplained variance share falling from $5.9\%$ to $3.0\%$ and the gross residual from $20.2\%$ to
$16.7\%$, which is what should happen if the removed component was genuinely spurious. The multiplicative variant agrees
throughout.

These are reported as a robustness check and not as the paper's headline, for a reason worth stating. The adjustment is
constructed from the mean path of the whole sample, so it uses information that was not available at any point during the days it
corrects, and a hedger running this strategy in real time would face the raw index and its drift. It also does not repair the
underlying defect identified in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}: centring the proxy does not make it informative about the
remaining session, since by the final bar only $0.3\%$ of its window bears on the contract at all. The claim supported here is
narrower and more useful than a corrected headline would be, namely that the proxy has a known and measurable defect, that the
defect accounts for the drifts and for nothing else, and that the attribution this paper reports is unchanged by it.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Four limits on the scope of these results should be stated plainly. The option studied is at the money at inception on every one
of the 57 days, so the attribution is an at-the-money result and does not carry to the out-of-the-money strikes that make up much
of 0DTE volume. The at-the-money case was chosen deliberately: it is where dealer gamma exposure concentrates~\cite{dimeraker2023},
where the hedging problem is most acute, and where the Gamma-dominance result is most cleanly interpretable. Out-of-the-money
strikes, which carry proportionally more Vega and Vanna, are left to future work. The parameters are a single uncalibrated set;
the $\kappa$ and $\epsilon$ sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:results} show Gamma stays largest throughout but that its precise share depends on $\epsilon$,
and no sweep over $\eta$, $\rho$, or $\lambda$ was attempted. The variance path is a one-day risk-neutral proxy standing in for an
instantaneous physical variance: it runs about a third above the realized intraday variance, which flatters the Gamma share, and
it carries the intraday roll-up documented in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, which manufactures the Vega term's drift.
Section~\ref{sec:detrend} removes the roll and finds the attribution unchanged, but that adjustment is estimated from the whole
sample rather than available in real time, and it centres the proxy without making it informative about the remaining session. A
variance path read from the expiring contract's own option prices is the real fix and needs data this paper does not have.
Finally, the two
attribution metrics measure different things and neither is complete on its own: the gross share records the size of a term's
movement without regard to the realized error, while the variance share records covariance with the realized error and so is
silent about terms that move a great deal without contributing to it.

The model is limited by the accuracy of the Heston parameters, an issue one can easily fix with better data. Another limitation is found in the premise itself:
all Greeks are unhedgeable in the terminal interval, because there exists no rebalance prior to expiry. Given that, market makers and retail traders alike should be
wary of 0DTE options, which are risky and difficult to hedge, especially as they near expiration.
This paper locates the hedging error and does not provide a definitive solution to the problem. To make this model better, a Milstein scheme \cite{gatheral2006} could be used to simulate the variance process, which would provide a more accurate
representation of the underlying asset's volatility.

A further limitation is structural. Heston is a pure-diffusion model, so the decomposition carries no jump term; any intraday jump in the underlying enters only
through $(\Delta S)^2$ and is therefore absorbed into the Gamma term, indistinguishable from ordinary diffusive movement. For 0DTE the omission is costly.
Bo\v{z}ovi\'c (2025)~\cite{bozovic2025}, working with five-minute SPY options, estimates the implied jump-risk premium at nearly twice the combined diffusion and volatility premia,
with jump intensity clustering at the open and close. The Gamma share reported above should therefore be read as diffusion and jump risk combined;
isolating the two would require adding a jump term to the decomposition. The bipower-variation check of Section~\ref{sec:results} bounds how much this
can matter, showing the attribution essentially unchanged on the subsample of days with no detectable jump activity, but bounding the contamination is
not the same as decomposing it, and a jump-diffusion version of this exercise remains the cleaner answer.


\section{Conclusion}
Learning the different attributions of the error is important for understanding the risks of 0DTE options and
using them effectively. Gamma carries the largest share on both measures reported here, two-thirds of the variance of the realized
hedged P\&L and half of the gross term movement, and the model conflates that share with jumps; a bipower-variation check on the
jump-free days leaves it intact. Vega is a clear second rather than a rounding error, taking about a third on both measures. It is
the variance risk a delta-only hedge cannot remove at any rebalancing frequency, and its gross share more than doubles from the
calmest to the most turbulent days, a pattern that survives a re-sort on the underlying's own move rather than the variance proxy's.
The other Greeks, while not negligible, are relatively small; Vanna is the instructive case, moving a great deal without
contributing to the error that is realized.
These results are preliminary. They describe at-the-money calls at a single baseline parameter set, robust to the mean-reversion
rate but not to the vol-of-vol, with a one-day variance proxy that runs about a third above realized variance and that climbs
mechanically through the session. That climb manufactures the drift in the Vega term: removing it kills the drift outright and
leaves the shares where they were, so the attribution survives the defect even though the sample can say nothing about whether a
0DTE delta hedge loses money on average. Further work is needed before the
attribution can be claimed for 0DTE hedging in general.
Future work should focus on reading the variance path from the expiring contract's own option prices rather than from an index of the wrong horizon, improving the Heston parameter estimates, adding a jump term to the decomposition, and testing the model on a wider range of data.

\section{Glossary of Variables}

The sensitivity Greeks ($\Delta$, $\Gamma$, $\nu$, $\Theta$, and the higher-order terms) are
defined in Section~1. The symbols below collect the model, market, and hedging notation that the
Heston and derivation sections introduce along the way.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Underlying and market.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $S$: price of the underlying asset (the spot), rescaled so that each trading day opens at $1$.
    \item $v$: instantaneous variance of the underlying; the volatility is $\sqrt{v}$.
    \item $\sigma$: volatility, with $\sigma = \sqrt{v}$; used in the Greek definitions of Section~1.
    \item $t$: calendar time. Written as a prefix, $\Delta t$ is a single hedging interval (one five-minute bar in the test).
    \item $\mu$: real-world (physical) drift of the underlying.
    \item $r$: risk-free interest rate ($r = 0.045$ in the test).
    \item $X,\,W$: the Brownian motions driving the price and the variance, correlated by $dX\,dW = \rho\,dt$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Heston parameters.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\kappa$: rate at which the variance reverts toward its long-run level.
    \item $\eta$: long-run (mean) level of the variance.
    \item $\epsilon$: volatility of volatility.
    \item $\rho$: correlation between the price and variance shocks.
    \item $\lambda$: market price of volatility risk; $\lambda = 0$ in the test, so the physical and risk-neutral variance drifts coincide.
    \item $\nu_{\mathrm{Q}}$: risk-neutral drift of the variance, $\nu_{\mathrm{Q}} = \kappa(\eta - v) - \lambda v$ (distinct from Vega $\nu$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Option and hedge portfolio.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V$: value of the option being hedged (a fresh at-the-money 0DTE call).
    \item $V_1$: value of the second option held to hedge variance (Vega) risk.
    \item $\Pi$: value of the delta--vega hedged portfolio, $\Pi = V - \Delta S - \Delta_1 V_1$.
    \item $\Delta$: units of the underlying held in the hedge, $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S - \Delta_1\,\partial V_1/\partial S$. Written as a prefix ($\Delta S$, $\Delta v$, $\Delta\Pi$) it instead denotes the change in a quantity over one hedging interval.
    \item $\Delta_1$: units of the hedging option $V_1$ held, chosen to cancel the portfolio's net Vega, $\Delta_1 = (\partial V/\partial v)\big/(\partial V_1/\partial v)$.
\end{itemize}
